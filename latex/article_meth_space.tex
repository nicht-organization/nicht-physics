% ABSTRACT
% ============================================================

\begin{abstract}
This paper introduces a minimal distinction between two modes of
consistency.

\emph{1-Consistency} concerns consistency within a specified
formal system and its admitted rules and operations.
\emph{0-Consistency} asks an additional question: whether the
description remains structurally adequate when a relevant
null, negating, inverse, complementary, or otherwise opposing
operation is admitted for examination.

The distinction does not replace classical logic or mathematics.
It provides an alternative perspective for investigating
structures that are consistent under one set of operations but
are not closed under a relevant inversion.

The paper deliberately does not identify all forms of negation,
nor does it identify zero with infinity or any particular
mathematical objects with ``Nothing''. A concrete negation is
always dependent on the object, language, structure, and
perspective in which it is formed.

The purpose of this paper is therefore only to define a
reusable distinction. Its consequences are left to subsequent
domain-specific investigations.
\end{abstract}

% ============================================================
\section{Introduction}
% ============================================================

Mathematical reasoning normally begins by fixing a structure,
its objects, its relations, and its permitted operations.

Once these have been fixed, consistency can be investigated
within the resulting framework. This mode of reasoning is
fundamental to mathematics and is not questioned here.

A second question can nevertheless be asked.

A system may be internally consistent while a relevant operation
suggested by its own structure is not available within the
system. Arithmetic over the natural numbers provides an
elementary example:

\[
2+3=5,
\]

while

\[
2-3=-1\notin\mathbb{N}.
\]

The first statement does not become false because the second
operation leaves the domain. Rather, the domain is not closed
under additive inversion.

The resulting distinction is the starting point of this paper:

\[
\boxed{
\text{consistency within a system}
\neq
\text{closure under every relevant inversion}.
}
\]

The notation ``0'' and ``1'' used below is therefore not intended
as a ranking of mathematical value. It denotes two different
directions of examination.

% ============================================================
\section{Two Modes of Consistency}
% ============================================================

Let

\[
S=(X,\mathcal{O},\mathcal{R})
\]

denote a formal system with domain $X$, admitted operations
$\mathcal{O}$, and rules or relations $\mathcal{R}$.

\subsection{1-Consistency}

We call $S$ \emph{1-consistent}, with respect to a specified
formal interpretation, when its permitted derivations and
operations do not produce a contradiction within that
interpretation.

In its simplest logical form:

\[
S\vdash P
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
S\nvdash\neg P
\]

for propositions for which the relevant notion of contradiction
is defined.

The definition is deliberately local to the chosen formal
framework.

1-Consistency therefore answers:

\[
\boxed{
\text{``Does the system remain consistent under its stated rules?''}
}
\]

It does not answer whether those rules constitute the most
appropriate or most complete representation of the object under
investigation.

\subsection{0-Consistency}

Let $I$ denote a relevant inversion operation associated with the
structure under investigation.

The operation $I$ is not assumed to be universal. It may take
different forms in different domains.

We call a system \emph{0-consistent}, with respect to $I$, when
the system's consistency can be examined without excluding the
structurally relevant inverse, null, complementary, or opposing
operation merely because it is not already contained in the
original description.

A minimal closure question is:

\[
\boxed{
I(\mathcal{O})\subseteq\mathcal{O}\;?
}
\]

If the answer is negative, the original system may remain
1-consistent while failing to be closed under the investigated
inversion.

The resulting extension may be written schematically as

\[
S\subseteq S^{I}.
\]

0-Consistency therefore asks:

\[
\boxed{
\text{``What happens to the consistency of the description when\\
its relevant inversion is admitted?''}
}
\]

This is not a stronger version of 1-Consistency. It is a
different test.

% ============================================================
\section{Inversion and Negation}
% ============================================================

The word ``inversion'' is used here deliberately more broadly
than formal logical negation.

In classical propositional logic one may have

\[
P\longmapsto\neg P.
\]

In arithmetic, an additive inverse is

\[
a\longmapsto-a,
\]

while a multiplicative inverse is

\[
a\longmapsto\frac{1}{a}.
\]

In set theory, relative to a universe $U$, a complement may be
written

\[
A\longmapsto U\setminus A.
\]

These operations are not identical.

The framework therefore does \emph{not} postulate a universal
operator called ``the negation''.

Instead, for each investigated structure we specify a concrete
operation

\[
I_X
\]

and examine its consequences.

In particular,

\[
I_{\mathrm{Thomas}}(\mathrm{Thomas})
\neq
I_{\mathrm{Banana}}(\mathrm{Banana})
\]

need not be interpreted as a contradiction. ``Not Thomas'' and
``not Banana'' describe different complements or counterpositions
relative to different predicates and domains.

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
\text{the operation may be structurally comparable\\
 without its result being identical across domains.}
}
\]

% ============================================================
\section{Minimal Mathematical Examples}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Logic}

For a proposition $P$, ordinary negation gives

\[
P\longmapsto\neg P.
\]

The relevant inversion is explicit. The question of
0-Consistency is therefore not whether $\neg P$ exists as a
logical symbol, but what happens when a system's treatment of
$P$ is examined together with the corresponding counterposition.

The precise behavior depends on the logical system and is not
assumed to be identical across all logics.

\subsection{Arithmetic}

Consider

\[
S_1=(\mathbb{N},+).
\]

The operation $+$ is closed:

\[
a,b\in\mathbb{N}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
a+b\in\mathbb{N}.
\]

The additive inverse is not:

\[
a,b\in\mathbb{N}
\quad\not\Longrightarrow\quad
a-b\in\mathbb{N}.
\]

For example,

\[
2-3=-1.
\]

The natural extension is

\[
\mathbb{N}\subset\mathbb{Z}.
\]

The original arithmetic is not thereby ``wrong''. Its
consistency under addition and its closure under additive
inversion are different properties.

\subsection{Zero and Inversion}

Zero is not infinity:

\[
0\neq\infty.
\]

Nevertheless, particular mathematical transformations relate
neighborhoods of zero to unbounded behavior. Under

\[
I(x)=\frac{1}{x},
\]

one has

\[
x\rightarrow0^+
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
I(x)\rightarrow+\infty
\]

and

\[
x\rightarrow0^-
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
I(x)\rightarrow-\infty.
\]

The relevant observation is therefore not an identification

\[
0=\infty,
\]

but a structural relationship under inversion.

\subsection{Sets}

The empty set is

\[
\varnothing.
\]

Relative to a universe $U$, complementation gives

\[
A\longmapsto U\setminus A.
\]

The complement of a singleton need not be ``another singleton''.
For example,

\[
U\setminus\{\mathrm{Thomas}\}
\]

may contain every element of $U$ except Thomas.

Thus the semantic or extensional size of a negation can be
dramatically different from that of the object being negated.

This illustrates why no universal size or meaning can be assigned
to ``negation'' independently of its domain.

% ============================================================
\section{Closure, Extension, and the Null Boundary}
% ============================================================

The preceding examples suggest a minimal distinction between
three questions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Is the original system consistent?
    \item Which operations are admitted by the system?
    \item What happens when a relevant inverse or complementary
          operation is considered?
\end{enumerate}

The first question belongs to 1-Consistency.

The third motivates 0-Consistency.

The second specifies the boundary between them.

This boundary is not assumed to be absolute. A system may be
closed under one operation and open under another.

For example,

\[
(\mathbb{N},+)
\]

is closed under addition but not under subtraction.

Likewise, a mathematical structure may be perfectly useful for
one purpose while being inadequate for another.

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
\text{non-closure is not automatically inconsistency.}
}
\]

Conversely,

\[
\boxed{
\text{consistency is not automatically closure under a relevant
extension.}
}
\]

This distinction is the central reusable element of the
framework.

% ============================================================
\section{Scope and the Necessary ``But''}
% ============================================================

The framework deliberately avoids turning any concrete
negation into an absolute ``Nothing''.

For any concrete object $X$,

\[
\neg X
\]

is generally a description of what is not $X$ relative to some
specified predicate, language, universe, or perspective.

Consequently,

\[
\neg X\neq\varnothing
\]

in general.

Likewise, repeated negation need not terminate in a privileged
absolute state:

\[
X
\longmapsto
\neg_X X
\longmapsto
\neg_{\neg_X X}(\neg_X X)
\longmapsto\cdots
\]

Each step may introduce a new distinction and therefore a new
``but''.

The present framework does not attempt to identify this sequence
with a metaphysical doctrine.

It merely preserves the methodological boundary:

\[
\boxed{
\text{a concrete negation is not the absolute Nothing.}
}
\]

This distinction is important both for mathematical and
empirical applications.

The same applies to zero and infinity. They may exhibit
remarkably related behavior under particular transformations
without becoming identical objects.

% ============================================================
\section{What This Framework Does Not Prescribe}
% ============================================================

This paper does not prescribe a replacement for classical
logic.

It introduces an alternative perspective on consistency.

It does not attempt to refute classical mathematics.

It treats established mathematics as both a working foundation
and an object that may legitimately be questioned.

It does not restrict the framework to metaphysical objects.
The same distinction may be investigated in mathematical,
computational, physical, biological, social, economic, or other
systems.

It does not claim to prove a particular open mathematical
problem.

Nor does it prescribe that a particular problem must have a
solution under this framework.

Instead, subsequent papers may ask whether changing the
perspective from which a problem is represented reveals useful
structure, invariants, obstructions, or solution strategies.

In particular, the present paper does not assume that all
negations are the same operation, that zero and infinity are
identical, or that any particular inversion must produce a
particular result.

These questions belong to the individual application.

% ============================================================
\section{Conclusion}
% ============================================================

The framework introduced here is intentionally small.

Given a formal system

\[
S=(X,\mathcal{O},\mathcal{R}),
\]

1-Consistency asks whether the system is consistent under its
specified interpretation and operations.

0-Consistency adds a second examination: identify a relevant
inversion, null, complementary, or opposing operation and ask
whether the description remains structurally adequate when that
operation is admitted.

The two questions are therefore:

\[
\boxed{
\text{1C: consistency within the given frame}
}
\]

and

\[
\boxed{
\text{0C: consistency under examination of the relevant
counter-frame}
}
\]

Neither question is declared universally superior.

The framework provides a distinction, not a predetermined
conclusion.

Its usefulness must therefore be established, if at all, by
application.

The following papers apply the same minimal distinction to
specific mathematical problems. They are free to confirm,
modify, restrict, or reject the usefulness of the framework in
their respective domains.

\[
\boxed{
\text{Paper 0 defines the question.\\
The subject papers are allowed to answer it differently.}
}
\]
