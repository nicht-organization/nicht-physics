% ABSTRACT
% ============================================================

\begin{abstract}
This paper introduces a minimal distinction between two modes of
consistency.

\emph{1-Consistency} denotes the ordinary internal consistency of a
formal system under its specified rules and operations, without
reference to any external or dual interpretation.
\emph{0-Consistency} denotes the consistency of a system under
unlimited iteration of a relevant self-negation or inversion
operator. It asks whether the system can sustain arbitrarily many
applications of such an operator without collapsing into
contradiction or triviality.

The two notions are dual in the sense that the assignment of the
logical values $0$ and $1$ to truth and falsehood is arbitrary as
long as a negation operation is present; swapping the labels does
not alter the underlying logical structure. Both modes therefore
yield equivalent first-order logical frameworks when the underlying
system is closed under the relevant negation. The difference arises
only when the system is not closed under arbitrary iteration of
that negation.

The paper does not identify any particular mathematical object with
a metaphysical absolute, nor does it introduce notions such as
eternity or temporal flow. It provides only a formal, reusable
distinction between two ways of examining consistency with respect
to a self-negation operation. Consequences are left to
domain-specific investigations.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% ============================================================

Mathematical reasoning normally begins by fixing a structure, its
objects, its relations, and its permitted operations. Once these
have been fixed, consistency can be investigated within the
resulting framework. This mode of reasoning is fundamental and is
not questioned here.\cite{godel1931formal, tarski1936begriff}

A second question can nevertheless be asked. A system may be
internally consistent while the operation of self-negation,
suggested by the system's own structure, is not available within
the system or is not closed under arbitrary iteration.

Consider the natural numbers with addition: the statement
\(2+3=5\) is valid, but the inverse operation \(2-3=-1\) leaves the
domain. The original system is consistent under addition, but not
closed under additive inversion. This illustrates that internal
consistency and closure under a relevant inversion are distinct
properties.

The resulting distinction is the starting point of this paper:

\[
\boxed{
\text{consistency within a system}
\neq
\text{consistency under arbitrary iteration of a relevant inversion}.
}
\]

The notation ``0'' and ``1'' used below is not intended as a
ranking of mathematical value. It denotes two different directions
of examination, dual to each other.

% ============================================================
\section{Two Modes of Consistency}
% ============================================================

Let
\[
S=(X,\mathcal{O},\mathcal{R})
\]
denote a formal system with domain \(X\), admitted operations
\(\mathcal{O}\), and rules or relations \(\mathcal{R}\).

\subsection{1-Consistency}

We call \(S\) \emph{1-consistent}, with respect to a specified
formal interpretation, when its permitted derivations and
operations do not produce a contradiction within that
interpretation.

In its simplest logical form:
\[
S\vdash P
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
S\nvdash\neg P
\]
for propositions for which the relevant notion of contradiction is
defined.

The definition is deliberately local to the chosen formal
framework. 1-Consistency therefore answers:
\[
\boxed{
\text{``Does the system remain consistent under its stated rules?''}
}
\]
It does not ask whether those rules are the most appropriate or
whether the system is closed under operations not contained in the
original description.

\subsection{0-Consistency}

Let \(I\) denote a relevant self-negation or inversion operator
associated with the structure under investigation. The operator is
\textit{not} assumed to be universal; it is specified
concretely for each domain. We assume that \(I\) is an endomorphism
on some extended domain, or at least can be iterated.

We call the system \emph{0-consistent} with respect to \(I\) if it
remains consistent under arbitrary finite iteration of \(I\). That
is, for every natural number \(n\),
\[
I^{n}(S)
\]
does not produce a contradiction, where \(I^{n}\) denotes the
\(n\)-fold application of \(I\) to the system, appropriately
defined. In many cases \(I\) acts on the domain and the rules, so
we require that the extended system
\[
S^{\omega}_{I} = \bigcup_{n \in \mathbb{N}} I^{n}(S)
\]
is consistent.

0-Consistency therefore asks:
\[
\boxed{
\text{``What happens to the consistency of the description when\\
its relevant self-negation is iterated arbitrarily often?''}
}
\]
This is not a stronger version of 1-Consistency; it is a different
test. A system may be 1-consistent but not 0-consistent, or vice
versa, depending on the nature of \(I\).

\subsection{Duality of the Two Modes}

The labels ``0'' and ``1'' are arbitrary. In classical two-valued
logic, one may assign \(0\) to false and \(1\) to true, or
conversely \(0\) to true and \(1\) to false. As long as a negation
operation \(\neg\) is present with \(\neg 0 = 1\) and
\(\neg 1 = 0\), the resulting logical structure is identical up to
relabeling. This is the sense in which 1-Consistency and
0-Consistency are dual: they are the same consistency notion viewed
from opposite sides of the truth-value assignment.

However, 0-Consistency as defined above is not merely the dual
interpretation of 1-Consistency; it concerns the closure of the
system under repeated application of a self-negation operator,
which goes beyond static relabeling. The duality manifests in the
fact that if the operator \(I\) is an involution (i.e.,
\(I^{2} = \text{id}\)), then 0-consistency reduces to a condition
on the extended system that is symmetrical with respect to the
original system.

% ============================================================
\section{Inversion and Negation}
% ============================================================

The word ``inversion'' is used deliberately more broadly than
formal logical negation. In classical propositional logic one may
have \(P \mapsto \neg P\). In arithmetic, an additive inverse is
\(a \mapsto -a\), while a multiplicative inverse is
\(a \mapsto 1/a\). In set theory, relative to a universe \(U\), a
complement may be written \(A \mapsto U \setminus A\).

These operations are not identical. The framework therefore does
not postulate a universal operator called ``the negation''.
Instead, for each investigated structure we specify a concrete
operation \(I_X\) and examine its consequences. The iteration of
such an operation is what underlies 0-Consistency.

An elementary example is the operation \(I(x) = -x\) on the
integers. It is an involution: \(I^{2}(x) = x\). The system
\((\mathbb{Z}, +, \cdot)\) is 0-consistent with respect to this
\(I\) because arbitrary iteration does not lead to contradiction.
In contrast, the system \((\mathbb{N}, +)\) is 0-inconsistent
with respect to the same \(I\), because the domain is not closed
under the operation.

The recursion ``not not'', or equivalently the iterated application
of a self-negation, is central. A system that can sustain
arbitrarily many applications of its relevant negation without
collapse exhibits a form of inner infinite capacity. This is what
0-Consistency captures.

% ============================================================
\section{Minimal Mathematical Examples}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Logic}

For a proposition \(P\), ordinary negation gives
\(P \mapsto \neg P\). The relevant inversion is explicit. The
question of 0-Consistency is whether the logical system can
accommodate arbitrary iterations of negation without producing a
contradiction. In classical logic, double negation is equivalent to
identity, so the iteration is periodic; the system is trivially
0-consistent. In non-classical logics, the situation may differ.

\subsection{Arithmetic}

Consider \(S_1 = (\mathbb{N}, +)\). The operation \(+\) is closed,
but the additive inverse is not:
\[
a,b \in \mathbb{N} \not\Longrightarrow a-b \in \mathbb{N}.
\]
The natural extension is \(\mathbb{N} \subset \mathbb{Z}\). The
original arithmetic is 1-consistent but 0-inconsistent with
respect to additive inversion. The extension \((\mathbb{Z}, +)\)
is 0-consistent, since \(-(-a) = a\) and the domain is closed under
the operation.

\subsection{Zero and Inversion}

Zero is not infinity: \(0 \neq \infty\). Nevertheless, the
transformation \(I(x) = 1/x\) maps neighborhoods of zero to
unbounded regions. Iterating \(I\) on a nonzero number yields
\[
I^{2}(x) = x,
\]
so the operation is an involution on \(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}\).
The relevant 0-consistency of a structure involving this inversion
depends on whether the domain is closed under the operation; for
example, the extended real line with \(I(0) = \infty\) is not a
field in the usual sense, but the mapping can be consistently
iterated in projective geometry.

\subsection{Sets}

Relative to a universe \(U\), complementation
\(A \mapsto U \setminus A\) is an involution. The system of all
subsets of \(U\) is 0-consistent with respect to complementation,
since \(U \setminus (U \setminus A) = A\). This holds for arbitrary
sets and does not require any metaphysical interpretation.

% ============================================================
\section{Closure, Extension, and the Null Boundary}
% ============================================================

The preceding examples suggest a minimal distinction between three
questions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Is the original system consistent?
    \item Which operations are admitted by the system?
    \item What happens when a relevant self-negation is iterated
          arbitrarily often?
\end{enumerate}

The first question belongs to 1-Consistency. The third motivates
0-Consistency. The second specifies the boundary between them.

A system may be closed under one operation and open under another.
For example, \((\mathbb{N}, +)\) is closed under addition but not
under subtraction. Therefore:
\[
\boxed{
\text{non-closure under an operation is not automatically
inconsistency.}
}
\]
Conversely,
\[
\boxed{
\text{consistency is not automatically closure under arbitrary
iteration of a relevant self-negation.}
}
\]
This distinction is the central reusable element of the framework.

% ============================================================
\section{Scope and Formal Limitations}
% ============================================================

The framework deliberately avoids turning any concrete negation into
an absolute ``Nothing''. For any concrete object \(X\), the
expression \(\neg X\) is generally a description of what is not
\(X\) relative to some specified predicate, language, universe, or
perspective. Consequently, \(\neg X \neq \varnothing\) in general.

Likewise, repeated negation need not terminate in a privileged
absolute state:
\[
X \mapsto \neg_X X \mapsto \neg_{\neg_X X}(\neg_X X) \mapsto \cdots
\]
Each step may introduce a new distinction and therefore a new
``but''. The present framework does not attempt to identify this
sequence with a metaphysical doctrine. It merely preserves the
methodological boundary:
\[
\boxed{
\text{a concrete negation is not the absolute Nothing.}
}
\]
This distinction is important both for mathematical and empirical
applications.

The framework does not prescribe a replacement for classical logic,
nor does it claim to prove any particular mathematical statement.
It introduces an alternative perspective on consistency, one that
focuses on the capacity of a system to sustain iterative
self-negation. Whether this perspective yields useful insights in
specific domains is left to subsequent investigations.

\section{The Universal Self-Negation Axiom}
% ============================================================

The preceding discussion treated the self-negation operator \(I\) as
concrete and domain-specific. We now introduce a stronger, formal
postulate that underlies the entire framework.

\begin{axiom}[Universal Self-Negation]
There exists a universal self-negation operator
\[
\mathcal{I}: \mathbf{Sys} \to \mathbf{Sys},
\]
where \(\mathbf{Sys}\) denotes the class of all formal systems,
such that for every formal system \(S\), the restriction
\(\mathcal{I}|_{S}\) is a relevant inversion for \(S\). Moreover,
\(\mathcal{I}\) is an involution on the meta-level:
\[
\mathcal{I}^{2} = \text{id}_{\mathbf{Sys}}.
\]
\end{axiom}

This axiom states that the ability to negate, invert, or complement
is not an optional feature limited to particular domains, but a
universal operation applicable to any formal system. The earlier
remark that \(I\) is ``not assumed to be universal'' was a
deliberate rhetorical device to avoid premature metaphysical
commitment; however, within the full framework presented here, the
universality of self-negation is taken as axiomatic.

With this axiom in place, 0-Consistency can be reformulated
uniformly: a system \(S\) is 0-consistent precisely when it is
consistent under arbitrary finite iteration of
\(\mathcal{I}|_{S}\). The earlier concrete examples are then
instances of this universal operator restricted to particular
domains.

% ============================================================
\section{Conclusion}
% ============================================================

The framework introduced here is intentionally small. Given a
formal system \(S=(X,\mathcal{O},\mathcal{R})\),
1-Consistency asks whether the system is consistent under its
specified interpretation and operations.\cite{wittgenstein1922tractatus}
0-Consistency asks whether the system remains consistent under
unlimited iteration of a relevant self-negation operator.

The two questions are therefore:
\[
\boxed{
\text{1C: consistency within the given frame}
}
\]
and
\[
\boxed{
\text{0C: consistency under arbitrary iteration of the relevant
self-negation}
}
\]
Neither question is declared universally superior. The framework
provides a distinction, not a predetermined conclusion. Its
usefulness must be established by application.

The assignment of the labels \(0\) and \(1\) to the two modes is
arbitrary and dual; swapping them yields the same logical structure
up to renaming. The core content of the distinction lies in the
iteration of the self-negation operator, not in the particular
symbols chosen.

Furthermore, the Universal Self-Negation Axiom makes explicit that
the operation of inversion is available across all formal systems,
thereby rendering the 0-Consistency question universally
applicable.

\[
\boxed{
\text{Paper 0 defines the question.\\
The subject papers are allowed to answer it differently.}
}
\]
